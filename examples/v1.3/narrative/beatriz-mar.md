# The Thread in Her Hands

A romantic period reconstruction of Beatriz Mar’s life. All people, places and records in this exercise are fictional training material. Only the supplied case and source extracts were consulted. Notes identify each paragraph’s basis; no external historical context was researched.

**Historical fiction / novelized reconstruction: paragraphs 4, 6 and 8 contain imagined scenes. Record statements and the grandson’s recollection remain separately identified; the case’s conclusions are provisional.**

Beatriz Mar comes into these surviving extracts through a date and a place: 3 May 1884, Vila Clara. The birth register records them beside her name. Of the child herself, these few words offer no portrait; her childhood lies beyond the supplied pages. [^1]

In 1907, a marriage entry names Beatriz Mar as a seamstress, aged twenty-five, marrying Tomás Neri. The occupation lends a human detail to the marriage entry: a skill named beside the names of bride and groom. [^2]

Yet the years refuse to meet neatly. A woman born in May 1884 would have been twenty-two or twenty-three in 1907, depending on the wedding date. The discrepancy remains unresolved; even the thread of her age cannot be drawn straight through these records. [^3]

Imagine her beside a window, a length of blue cloth gathered across her knees. Afternoon light touches the needle and withdraws; beneath her fingers, the fold settles into place. She bends closer, drawing the thread through until its small loop disappears. Outside the imagined room, the day goes on without asking what she has made of it. Within, one edge has become even, one loose thing secure. She lays her palm over the seam, feeling for what the eye might miss. The cloth makes a soft whisper as she lifts it. For this imagined moment, the narrow seam is enough to hold her whole attention. [^4]

The household extract of 1910 brings three names together at Rua do Poço 8: Beatriz Mar, Tomás Neri, and a daughter, Rosa, aged two. The address gives their appearance in the record a particular doorway, though the extract describes no room behind it. [^5]

Picture an evening there: Rosa watching a ribbon slide between Beatriz’s hands, Tomás turning towards them as the child laughs. A curl of ribbon falls across the table. Beatriz lets it rest a moment before winding it away. In this invented stillness, affection needs no grand declaration; it takes the shape of a shared glance, a little space made for a child’s delight. The ribbon glimmers faintly against the wood. For an instant, its trailing end resembles a path too narrow for anyone but Rosa’s small finger to follow. [^6]

A grandson remembered that Beatriz taught neighbourhood children to sew. His account supplies no date for those lessons. He did not know when she died. What reaches us through his memory is the teaching, without the sound of her voice or the words she used. [^7]

Let the last imagined scene remain unfinished: a child’s uneven stitches, Beatriz waiting beside them, the needle poised for another attempt. No farewell enters this room. There is only the thread, catching the light, and the small interval before the hand begins again. [^8]
