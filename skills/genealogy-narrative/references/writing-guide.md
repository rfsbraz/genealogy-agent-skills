# Writing a life as a story

## Aim

Give the reader a sense of a life unfolding in a particular place and time. Use names, work, households, distances, signatures, addresses and objects that actually appear in the evidence. Let gaps shape the story instead of hiding them. A brief life with few records can still be moving.

Interpret “romantic” as lyrical or evocative unless the user specifically asks for a love story. Do not force romance, tragedy, heroism or a moral lesson. Let a documented ordinary detail carry emotion: a repeated address, an unsteady signature, the same witness at two marriages. Do not infer literacy from a single signature or lack of one without context.

## Shape

1. Open on a documented moment or the surviving record itself.
2. Move through the person's life with clear transitions and an honest chronology. If the date is approximate, keep that visible.
3. Weave in relevant, sourced context. Separate “people in this district faced...” from “she personally experienced...”.
4. Keep conflicts visible at the point they matter. Two possible fathers cannot become one convenient character.
5. End with what the evidence leaves us: a trace, a relationship, a change, or an unresolved question. Avoid invented final words or a deathbed scene.

Prefer concrete language and varied sentence length. Use flourish selectively. Avoid repetitive “little did they know”, “history would remember”, “a testament to”, generic mist/cobblestones, and modern psychological diagnoses. Use real archival quotations only when verified and cited; mark modern translations.

## Three examples (fictional training material)

Assume a synthetic fixture says only: “A 1901 marriage entry describes Ada Vale as a seamstress. Her birth year remains uncertain.”

**Documentary:** “The marriage entry of 1901 describes Ada Vale as a seamstress. It gives us an occupation and a place in the record, but it does not settle the year she was born.”

**Evocative:** “Ada enters this part of the surviving record through the spare language of a marriage entry: a name, a date, an occupation. Seamstress. The word offers a glimpse of her working life; the year of her birth remains beyond our certainty.”

This is flourish grounded in the record. It does not invent the sound of her sewing machine or her feelings about marriage.

**Novelized:** “Imagine Ada setting her needle beside the folded cloth and pausing at the window. For a moment the room is still.”

Label that paragraph “Imagined scene: the room, needle, gesture and pause are literary reconstruction, not recorded events.” Introduce the entire work as a novelized reconstruction. Do not add a fictional spouse or actual date to the scene. Keep the record's occupation in a separate documented paragraph.

## Inference is different from invention

An inference has a stated evidential basis and reasoning: two addresses may suggest a move, but do not reveal its route or motive. A possibility about daily life needs context and qualified wording. “She must have been devastated” is invented inner experience, not an evidence-based inference. Using “perhaps” does not make an arbitrary scene historically grounded.

Imagined dialogue and thoughts belong only in novelized mode and in identified imagined blocks. A recollected quotation needs attribution to its actual source; an invented quotation can never appear as a historical quote or archival extract.

## Editorial checklist

- Every personal assertion maps to evidence, attributed memory or a visible inference.
- Historical context is local/temporal enough to matter and cites consulted material.
- Any invented sensory event, dialogue or inner life is novelized and identified.
- Contradictions and unknowns survive in the prose.
- No new person, relationship or consequential event has been invented.
- Notes let readers locate each kind of passage without reading implementation details.
- Publication scope and privacy were checked separately from story quality.

Do not claim GPS applies to imaginative flourishes. GPS governs the genealogical conclusions underlying the story; the literary layer remains a derived work.
